Neutrinos come in three mass states, and we still don't know how those states are
ordered. It sounds like bookkeeping. It isn't.

## The question

We know two mass-squared *splittings* fairly well, but not their sign relative to
each other. Either the third state is the heaviest (**normal ordering**) or the
lightest (**inverted ordering**). That single bit of information ripples into the
physics of the early universe, the prospects for detecting neutrinoless double
beta decay, and how we interpret every long-baseline oscillation measurement.

## Why long-baseline experiments can answer it

As neutrinos travel hundreds of kilometers through the Earth, matter effects nudge
oscillation probabilities differently depending on the ordering. DUNE's 1,300 km
baseline is long enough that the effect is large and clean — which is much of why
the experiment is designed the way it is.

That's the kind of thing I'd like to write about more: not just the result, but
the reasoning that makes a particular detector, at a particular distance, the right
tool for a particular question.
